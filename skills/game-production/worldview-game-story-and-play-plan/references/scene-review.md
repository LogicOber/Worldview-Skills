# Reviewing a scene before implementation

## The answer is visible immediately

A locked office has a key hanging beside it. Calling this a five-minute investigation misstates the experience. Either make it a brief introduction, or let obtaining the key create a real decision: its owner is using it, borrowing it changes their route, and the player can instead reach the office through a previously seen service hatch. Those consequences need cues before the choice.

Do not hide the key under a randomly chosen object to restore duration. Difficulty should arise from understanding the situation, not guessing the author's hiding place.

## The player knows the answer before the character

If two records visibly have the same origin, an attentive player may identify the unreliable evidence immediately. Let them compare the source marks and advance. The next scene can ask how to obtain an independent reading, who will accept it, or whether proving it exposes someone. Requiring seven more documents teaches that the interface ignores reasoning.

## Quiet rooms feel empty

After a stressful encounter, a worker asks for help returning a dropped lunch box. That brief action can locate a safe passage, reveal a relationship, and let the player see who was affected. Add the second purpose only if it fits the person; not every intimate moment must award a key. Test whether the player has a reason to stay and whether they can leave when ready.

## A twist requires a hidden rule

A sensor suddenly sees through a wall during the finale. If its earlier behavior established that walls block it, the reveal changes rules after the decision. Seed the different sensor or establish a networked observer earlier. The player must be able to distinguish “my understanding was incomplete” from “the author changed the game.”

## The same errand repeats

Three fuses from three distant rooms differ only by location. Keep one if the others add no new decision. Alternatives that earn their space include preserving a powered medical room while rerouting a lift, choosing a noisier manual route, or returning through a room changed by the first restoration. The objective then changes a person's circumstances as well as a door.

## The story depends on optional reading

A finale requires knowing that a lift ignores emergency badges. If the only explanation is a collectible maintenance memo, a player can reach the finale without the rule. Demonstrate it during mandatory play; let the memo explain the history or offer a shortcut. Optional detail rewards attention without making the ending incomprehensible for everyone else.

## A conversation stops play

Long dialogue can work when the player is judging someone. It becomes filler when both speakers state facts the player just proved. Replace recap with a disagreement, admission, request, or practical response. Allow pauses and resumable key lines; do not force a code to be read during a sprint.

## A deadline punishes curiosity

A story set before dawn does not automatically need a real-time countdown. Use authored changes at chapter events unless actual time management is the chosen mechanic. If a real timer is required, show its rule, account for reading and accessibility, and specify which activities pause it. A clock that secretly waits for the player should not claim simulated accuracy.

## A sole ending erases player decisions

Different routes can reach the same rescue with different knowledge, equipment, help, and relationships. Preserve those effects in the route and dialogue. Do not advertise contradictory final outcomes and then normalize them invisibly. If saving a character is mandatory, a failed rescue needs a retry or recoverable rescue path rather than an unannounced second ending.

## The climax depends on sudden competence

The final action should reuse an action already tried calmly and under modest pressure. Prepare its components separately so the finale can combine them. A late tool, unexplained authority, newly sympathetic machine, or perfect speech is not an earned solution.

## An emergency waits for the plot to catch up

A player reaches a manual stop beside an endangered person, but cannot use it until they collect three reports. Unless the machine has an established physical dependency, this is an errand imposed by the author. Let the player stop the immediate danger. Investigation can still matter for preventing a restart, finding other affected people, repairing the damage, or deciding who takes responsibility. Explain the difference between a local stop and a broader recovery; do not make paperwork a magical safety interlock.

## The estimate is precise but unsupported

A nine-minute chapter needs nine minutes of plausible play. Show what occupies the player, which actions overlap, and how the informed route differs. Replace a promised duration with an estimate and a validation question when no runtime exists. Never describe a desk walkthrough as observing real players.
