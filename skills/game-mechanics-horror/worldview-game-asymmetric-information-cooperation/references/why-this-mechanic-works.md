# Why asymmetric information becomes cooperation

## Difference alone does not create interdependence

Two players may see different screens and still play separate games. One may read a code while the other enters it, leaving the first without meaningful choices and the second without interpretation. The activity is divided, but the reasoning is not shared.

Cooperation appears when information and agency cross. One role can distinguish something the other cannot. The other can change something the first cannot. Both decide what matters, communicate through a shared vocabulary, and observe one consequence that updates their common model.

The important structure is therefore not “Player A sees X; Player B sees Y.” It is:

```text
fact -> partial cue -> interpretation -> message -> acknowledgement
     -> role-specific action -> shared consequence -> updated model
```

Every edge must work for the encounter to remain playable.

## Unique information needs unique agency

A passive observer often becomes a human instruction manual. An operator who only follows exact commands becomes a remote hand. Either role may feel replaceable.

Give the observer interpretation, selection, timing, or local risk. They may choose which clue matters, maintain a viewing instrument, move among sightlines, or decide when information is reliable. Give the operator order, position, resource, commitment, or local threat. They may decide which control to stage, when to ask for confirmation, or whether there is enough time to act.

Neither role needs equal actions every second. Across the complete loop, each must make a decision that affects the result.

## Vocabulary reduces bandwidth without removing thought

Under pressure, unlimited description becomes noisy. A compact vocabulary lets players compress information and notice misunderstanding. Stable names for locations, symbols, directions, rhythms, and states support that compression.

The vocabulary should be taught through shared low-pressure evidence before it becomes necessary. If the first appearance of an unfamiliar symbol is also an irreversible timed test, failure measures prior cultural knowledge or lucky wording rather than cooperation.

Accessible alternatives must preserve the same semantic distinctions. Replacing a red/green contrast with labeled shapes does not remove the mechanic; it exposes what the mechanic actually depends on.

## Acknowledgement makes misunderstanding observable

Communication failures are often silent. A player hears “left bell” but maps it to a different viewpoint. The action occurs, danger arrives, and neither knows whether the clue, message, input, network, or rule failed.

Acknowledgement externalizes the shared model before commitment. The receiver highlights a control, repeats a token, stages a preview, or sends a ping. The sender can correct it while the cost is still manageable.

Acknowledgement should be proportional. Confirming every footstep makes interaction bureaucratic. Confirming the valve that floods a room makes the consequence legible.

## Horror pressure should constrain attention, not erase the channel

A closing threat, intermittent power, local pursuit, or narrow window makes players prioritize what to say and when. That supports horror because communication itself consumes safety.

Noise that makes required speech literally unintelligible, latency that consumes the entire window, or cues available only through one unsupported sense do not create productive pressure. They make completion depend on equipment and circumstance outside the rule.

The communication budget must include perception, wording, channel delay, acknowledgement, physical action, and a safety margin. Expert developers who already know the answer will systematically underestimate it.

## Shared consequences close the loop

After the action, both roles need evidence about what changed. The observer may see the mark extinguish while the operator sees the gear lock; both receive a shared bell tone and objective phase. This lets them refine the next exchange.

If only the server knows success, players repeat inputs. If only one client sees it, their models diverge. Feedback can remain role-specific, but it must point to one authoritative transition.

## Cooperation includes task and last-known-state ownership

A companion or remote player becomes more than a quest marker when they have a current task, a reason, a required capability, a last verified location and condition, knowledge carried, and visible success, delay, injury, or failure transitions. Communication loss then creates a concrete question: are they still repairing the lift, did they retreat to the fallback node, or did the last acknowledged state expire?

Do not reveal a live position through UI after the fiction removes contact. Last known means last verified. Updating it requires direct sight, an accepted message, or another authoritative event the receiving role is permitted to know.

Physical possession and knowledge also differ. One participant can hold a tool without knowing its purpose; another can understand the target without being able to act. A magical shared journal erases that cooperation by giving every role the latest truth regardless of observation or transmission.

## Live cooperation differs from sequential viewpoint ownership

This method serves participants who are active in the same problem and must transmit or acknowledge information. A single player switching to a later protagonist creates a different persistence problem: earlier actions and knowledge must become later world state or evidence even when no live exchange occurs. Route that problem to the character-handoff Skill when available instead of stretching the co-op protocol across an asynchronous campaign.

The boundary matters for save/load. Live cooperation restores session roles, task state, messages, acknowledgements, and shared consequences. Sequential viewpoint design restores character chronology, residues, and later discovery conditions. Combining both in one ledger makes every character appear connected and omniscient.

## Failure should diagnose the broken edge

A good failure distinguishes wrong clue, ambiguous message, stale phase, incorrect control, late commit, or ignored threat. It need not expose the entire solution. It should tell the group which part of its process to change.

Immediate unexplained death encourages blame. A reversible rejection, resource cost, local threat increase, or later terminal state with a concise shared recap preserves learning and social safety.

## Network state and social communication are separate

The game server can validate objects and timers, but it cannot guarantee that people understood one another. Conversely, perfect voice communication cannot repair clients acting on different authoritative phases.

Both layers require contracts. Network messages need phase identifiers, idempotence, reconnect, and late-join behavior. Human messages need vocabulary, acknowledgement, accessible channels, and sufficient time.

## Counterexamples

Do not use this method when:

- everyone receives and can act on the same necessary information;
- secrecy and betrayal, rather than cooperation, are the intended engine;
- one player merely reads a complete answer to another;
- only voice can express a required distinction but the platform does not require voice;
- a single-player companion is expected to solve the reasoning invisibly;
- participant locations update through an omniscient quest marker after communication is lost;
- a shared journal grants observations that were never transmitted;
- the main problem is an earlier playable character leaving evidence for a later playable character rather than live cooperation;
- the project has no multiplayer or role-switching capability and only a design document is requested.

## The smallest complete proof

One clue, one message, one acknowledgement, one action, and one shared consequence can prove the loop. Test it from both sides, with a plausible misunderstanding, with a non-voice channel, and with a disconnect. More puzzle steps cannot repair a broken information edge.
