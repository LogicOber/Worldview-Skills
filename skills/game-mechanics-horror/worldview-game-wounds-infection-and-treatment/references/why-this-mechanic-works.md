# Why wounds and treatment work as unfinished decisions

Instant damage asks one question: did the harmful event reduce a number enough to end play? A wound system can ask a longer question. The event creates a condition that competes with route safety, supplies, time, and the need to reach a better treatment point. The player is not only surviving the impact but deciding how to carry its consequences.

That continuing state must remain understandable and recoverable. An opaque meter that produces sudden death does not create care or tension; it creates a hidden timer. A graphic effect without mechanical information may intensify presentation while preventing a reasoned choice.

## Separate dimensions create distinct interventions

A wound, contamination, infection progression, and whole-body strain do not need to be one value. In a fictional model, each dimension can support a different action. Stabilization controls one immediate pressure. Decontamination changes exposure. A later treatment addresses an established stage. Recovery advances under its own conditions.

This separation prevents one universal item from erasing every consequence and helps the player understand why an action worked. It also prevents the design from claiming that a single invented sequence represents real medicine.

## Time turns treatment into route planning

When a condition progresses under a declared clock, distance to safety matters. Portable action may buy enough time to reach a fixed station. Taking a short dangerous route can preserve resources; a longer quiet route may allow the state to advance. The treatment system therefore belongs to the map rather than only a status menu.

The clock must stop and start predictably. Progress during required menus, loading, pause, or application suspension can punish someone for operating the interface or living outside the game. Explicit time ownership makes both difficulty and accessibility testable.

## Feedback supports uncertainty without obscurity

The player does not need exact numeric biology. They do need to know whether the condition is stable, worsening, responding, or incompletely treated. Inspection text, patterned icons, optional animation, audio alternatives, and a condition history can communicate that trend.

Uncertainty may remain around how much route time is safe or which limited resource is best preserved. The system should not hide which fictional state an item affects or whether an action completed.

## Staged treatment gives supplies different values

Portable stabilization, a cleaning stage, protective covering, setting-specific treatment, reassessment, and rest can each have a distinct role. A player may spend a scarce portable resource immediately or accept pressure while traveling to a better-equipped location.

More stages are not automatically better. Each one must change a decision, provide readable feedback, and remain operable. Redundant clicks and pseudo-medical detail add friction and can falsely suggest realism.

## Escalation needs a recovery path

If one missed moment guarantees an unknown terminal outcome, players may reload rather than engage with the system. A readable intermediate state and a more expensive recovery preserve consequence while allowing learning. Severe outcomes can still exist at declared boundaries after warnings and failed opportunities.

This does not mean every route is equally easy. It means the player can connect outcome to state and action instead of guessing whether a hidden random event already doomed the run.

## Respect is a mechanical requirement

When illness is used as shorthand for evil or loss of humanity, the game borrows fear from real people and conditions. It can also make care appear futile or cruelty appear naturally correct. A fictional threat does not require that analogy.

The mechanic can locate horror in the dangerous organism, scarce infrastructure, uncertain route, institutional abandonment, or bodily vulnerability while retaining the person's identity and agency. Injured, infected, disabled, treated, and assisted characters remain people. Language, objectives, failure states, and enemy logic must all preserve that fact.

## Accessibility distinguishes decisions from impairment mimicry

Blurred screens, dropped inputs, intense pulsing, forced camera movement, and distorted audio are presentation devices, not the treatment decision itself. They may exclude players or trigger symptoms while conveying less information.

Reduced-intensity presentation, neutral status text, adjustable mechanical effects, and generous interaction timing preserve the causal model. They allow a player to reason about stabilization and route cost without being required to endure a simulated impairment.

## Persistence preserves causality

Wounds collect clocks, interventions, and partial progress. A save that restores the resource cost but not the treatment effect—or advances time twice on load—breaks the relationship the player learned. Stable identifiers and explicit transition time keep the condition coherent across restart and migration.

As with every survival system, trust in the state allows uncertainty in the fiction. The game may withhold what waits beyond a door; it must not lose track of whether treatment already occurred.
