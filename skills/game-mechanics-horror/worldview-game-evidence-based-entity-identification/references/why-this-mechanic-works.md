# Why entity identification must be an experiment loop

An unknown creature and a list of clues can create the appearance of investigation without requiring reasoning. If the player merely picks up three highlighted objects and the journal reveals a name, the unknown identity never affected how evidence was produced. The loop is collection followed by a quiz.

Evidence-based identification becomes a mechanic when the player can ask the world a bounded question, create the conditions under which an answer is meaningful, survive long enough to observe it, and use the result to distinguish candidates. The entity’s hidden identity determines capabilities; current behavior creates opportunities; valid tests produce observations; the player interprets them.

## A candidate matrix defines the reasoning space

The candidate matrix is not only an answer table. It determines whether investigation can succeed. If two candidates share every available result, no amount of careful play can distinguish them. If a candidate’s only unique evidence depends on an optional random event, some cases have no reliable solution.

Checking the matrix before implementation exposes these problems cheaply. Each candidate needs a practical distinguishing path. Not every evidence item needs to be unique by itself; combinations can be the point. The important condition is that an attainable accepted set narrows to the intended answer under declared rules.

The matrix also bounds scope. Three carefully different candidates and three meaningful tests can create more reasoning than a large taxonomy filled with near-duplicates.

## A result is meaningful only under valid conditions

Suppose a placed sensor shows no change. That absence may mean the entity lacks the associated property. It may also mean the entity never entered range, the sensor was not calibrated, the test ended too early, or another event interrupted it. Treating all absence as negative evidence rewards careless testing.

A protocol separates those outcomes. Preparation and eligibility establish that the question was asked correctly. The observation window gives the capable entity an opportunity to answer. Only then can no response become a valid negative.

Positive evidence also needs provenance. A dramatic meter spike beside a known machine may be contamination, not an entity property. Naming the contaminating source and giving the player a way to isolate it turns a false-looking result into a second experiment.

## Inconclusive is information about the test, not the entity

An inconclusive result tells the player that the procedure did not establish either answer. This may feel less rewarding than an immediate clue, but it protects the logic of the system. The player can improve position, wait for another state, recalibrate, or choose a different tool.

The game should explain why a test was inconclusive at the resolution appropriate to the fiction. It need not expose internal booleans, but “signal lost when you left the marked field” supports a better next attempt. A blank journal entry does not.

This distinction becomes especially important under threat pressure. Retreating before the observation window ends is often the correct survival decision. It should preserve previous accepted evidence while leaving the interrupted test unresolved.

## The entity should not emit an answer key

If all evidence appears immediately upon entering the room, tools become themed buttons. The selected identity defines what the entity can produce, while current state and the player’s setup determine whether that evidence becomes observable.

Different instruments can ask different questions. A placed field tests whether the entity crossing a boundary creates a response. A reflective setup tests how its form relates to an image. An environmental provocation tests a behavior at increased risk. These are distinct because they require different world arrangements and attention, not because their icons differ.

Random delay can create uncertainty, but unbounded withholding destroys inference. Every required result needs a controllable opportunity, a maximum valid window, or an alternate route to distinction.

## Pressure gives observation a cost

Investigation asks the player to look carefully. Horror pressure asks whether it is safe to keep looking. A tool may occupy vision, a placed sensor may need retrieval, or a provocation may increase danger. These costs make evidence acquisition part of play.

Pressure should not rewrite the hidden answer. When danger interrupts a test, the protocol classifies the result based on what actually occurred. The player may lose time or position, but not the logic needed to understand the case.

Accepted evidence normally survives retreat. This lets the investigation accumulate rather than forcing repetitive perfect runs. If the fiction permits evidence decay, that becomes an explicit timed record with visible consequences, not a silent journal deletion.

## The ledger separates observation from belief

Players may draw the wrong conclusion from valid evidence. That possibility is essential to deduction. A good ledger preserves what happened without forcing one interpretation too early.

Separating observed, interpreted, and hypothesized layers allows the interface to support different levels of assistance. One mode may automatically show compatible candidates. Another may leave filtering to the player. Both operate on the same accepted evidence and do not read the hidden answer.

Provenance makes disagreements inspectable in multiplayer. Two players can see that a reading was contaminated or that they observed the same test event rather than arguing over a duplicated checkbox.

## Diagnostic value matters more than clue volume

Ten notes that repeat “the entity is dangerous” do less investigative work than one access record that rules out a claimed location. A clue earns space by changing a candidate, prediction, route, tool, permission, or final action. Otherwise it is optional context and should not occupy the critical evidence path.

This applies to human and institutional traces as much as instruments. A key proves potential access, not actual entry. A payment proves a transaction, not motive. A schedule establishes an expected location, not an alibi unless the observation window and witness are valid. The ledger retains these limits so the notebook cannot turn correlation into certainty.

## Testimony can be sincere, useful, and wrong

A witness reports what they perceived from their position and history. Fear, partial access, old rules, loyalty, or a changed entity can make a sincere account incorrect. Store the claimant, declared basis, interest, time, place, and testable prediction. Then let behavior or residue support or contradict it.

Do not use one final testimony to overwrite everything the player observed. A sympathetic history may explain why a dangerous being acted, but it does not erase damage already established. Likewise, one harmful act does not prove every hostile claim about it. Keeping help and harm as separate evidence gives the player an operational relationship to choose: avoid, contain, expose, cooperate under a condition, or release after a concrete safeguard.

## Ownership is evidence only when its limits are explicit

Holding an object and understanding it are different. A person may possess a seal without knowing whom it protects; another may know the rule but lack access to act. The inventory or world system remains authoritative for possession, while the evidence ledger records who knew what and when. This prevents a shared notebook from turning every character omniscient or duplicating an item after a handoff.

## Commitment turns interpretation into consequence

Without commitment, players can click every candidate until one succeeds. Submission needs a rule and consequence. An early risky guess can be valid design; so can a minimum evidence threshold. What matters is that authority compares the choice to the actual case and does not allow the UI to set the answer.

A post-identification action closes the causal chain. Choosing the correct containment process, route, or defense demonstrates that identity mattered in the world. The answer screen alone proves only that a menu comparison worked.

## Failure and restart test the hidden lifecycle

Useful failures include staying in a test zone too long, acting on contaminated evidence, or committing a poorly supported identity. Each points back to information available in the mechanic. An unrelated random attack while a menu is open teaches nothing about investigation.

Restart must clear more than position. The hidden identity or seed, entity opportunity, instruments, active windows, delayed callbacks, ledger, contamination, hypotheses, and outcome all participate in the case. Distinguishing “retry this case” from “generate a new case” keeps testing and player expectation coherent.

## What may change without changing the mechanic

The unknown can be supernatural, biological, artificial, human, environmental, or entirely fictional. Evidence can be physical traces, instrument readings, behavior, language, spatial effects, or changes produced by an experiment. Pressure can come from the entity, another hazard, limited time, or resource exposure.

The reusable structure remains: multiple candidates begin plausible, valid protocols produce bounded observations, the ledger preserves facts without inventing them, pressure makes observation costly, and a supported commitment has a real consequence.

The method fails when the last document supplies an answer key, a face or silhouette is treated as identity proof, every witness repeats the same authorial truth, mixed behavior is flattened into innocence, or accepted evidence never changes what the player can do.
