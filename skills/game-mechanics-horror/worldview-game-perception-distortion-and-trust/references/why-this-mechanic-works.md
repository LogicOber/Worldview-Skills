# Why distortion needs reliable anchors

## Uncertainty is playable only when it has boundaries

Horror often benefits from incomplete information. Incomplete information asks the player to infer. Arbitrary information asks them to guess what the designer decided this time.

Perception distortion crosses that boundary easily. If any sound, label, route, objective, control, collision response, or system message can be false without a rule, no observation can improve the player's next decision. The game may surprise them, but it cannot be learned.

A trust mechanic therefore needs two simultaneous facts:

1. selected cues can become unreliable under declared conditions;
2. some method of reasoning remains reliable enough to test those cues.

The tension comes from using that method while exposure, time, or danger makes verification costly.

## Truth and presentation are different responsibilities

The authoritative simulation answers what is true: where the player stands, whether a door is open, which objective is complete, and whether a threat can make contact. Presentation answers how the player learns those facts.

Distortion belongs primarily in presentation. Changing a sign is not the same as moving the room. Showing a false reflection is not the same as spawning a threat. Muting a distant sound is not the same as deleting the event.

Keeping the layers explicit prevents accidental rule changes and makes tests possible. A test can assert that the exit remained east while presentation showed a false western label. A player can compare that label with a protected compass. The contradiction becomes authored evidence rather than a bug.

## An anchor creates agency without removing fear

A reliable anchor is sometimes mistaken for a solution that makes the encounter easy. It need not be. The compass may require a hand that would otherwise hold a light. A protected map may be readable only in exposed stations. A companion's code may consume scarce radio power. A physical landmark may require the player to stop and look behind them.

The anchor preserves the relationship between observation and action. Its cost preserves pressure.

An anchor must have a stable scope. A compass can verify direction, not whether the voice ahead is friendly. A hand mark can verify that the player visited a door, not whether the room behind it is unchanged. Narrow anchors encourage comparison instead of becoming a universal truth button.

## Authored false cues teach; noise merely accumulates

Random whispers, flicker, chromatic shifts, and camera noise can establish mood. They rarely create a decision by themselves. A false cue becomes mechanical when it proposes a specific mistaken action that an anchor could dispute.

For example, a changed room label invites a wrong turn. A delayed reflection suggests a threat is farther away. A repeated radio instruction encourages the player to restore the wrong relay. Each alternative can be authored around the current location, objective, and exposure tier.

Randomness may select among eligible alternatives, but context must decide what is legal. Otherwise the system eventually produces contradictions that have no meaning or destroy progression.

## Escalation needs a budget

If every effect activates at maximum intensity, the result is sensory accumulation. The player cannot identify which rule changed, accessibility degrades, and strong moments have no contrast.

A budget limits how many contradictions can be active and gives each tier a purpose. A low tier may add ambiguous sound without asserting a false location. A middle tier may alter one label. A high tier may combine one substitution with environmental suppression while preserving the anchor. Recovery then has an observable effect beyond changing a bar.

Hysteresis matters because exposure near a threshold otherwise causes cues to blink between meanings. Entry and exit conditions should be separated enough that a presentation state can complete coherently.

## System trust must remain outside the fiction

Games ask players to trust save confirmations, accessibility controls, pause states, purchases, content warnings, connection status, and platform dialogs. Faking those systems as horror effects risks real data loss, accessibility failure, or coercion.

The fiction can imitate an in-world terminal. It should not imitate the operating system or lie about whether progress was saved. This boundary is both an ethical rule and a technical one: players need a dependable way to stop, configure, and resume the experience.

## Representation is not a texture

Using psychiatric language as a synonym for supernatural evil turns real people into atmosphere and claims a realism the mechanic does not possess. A fictional force should be named by its fictional rule: signal saturation, dream pressure, memory bleed, spore exposure, or another precise term.

If a project intentionally represents a real condition, that is a different undertaking. It requires lived-experience and subject-matter review, not a generic horror mechanic. This Skill neither supplies nor substitutes for that work.

## Counterexamples

Do not use this method when:

- the only goal is a visual filter with no decision;
- all information must remain strictly reliable for the intended audience or context;
- the scene is a non-interactive film sequence;
- the design depends on undetectable random punishment;
- the requested subject is a real diagnosis requiring responsible representation;
- the effect must spoof real saves, platform UI, purchases, or accessibility controls.

## The smallest complete proof

One route can prove the mechanic. Establish a stable cue and anchor, introduce one caused distortion, let the player compare them, provide a costly recovery, and test both trusting and rejecting the false cue. If that loop is not readable and fair, more visual effects will not repair it.
