# Why sound detection and distraction works

## Sound turns consequence into a spatial prediction

The player acts now and predicts a response elsewhere. A footstep may pull a listener around a corner; a timed device may clear a junction; opening a loud mechanism may be safe only after another event has committed the threat. That delay between action and response gives sound strategic value.

The prediction depends on a stable causal chain. If the listener appears to know the player instead of the event, distraction is false. If map barriers affect audible presentation but not AI reception, the world is inconsistent. If identical events produce unexplained opposite reactions, planning gives way to trial and error.

The mechanic therefore benefits from being simpler than real acoustics. It does not need to reproduce wave physics. It needs to express a small set of spatial relationships accurately enough that players can learn, exploit, and diagnose them.

## Events protect the boundary between world truth and listener knowledge

The simulation knows where the player is. A listener that heard a dropped object knows that an event occurred at the object's location and time. Those are different facts.

Capturing an immutable origin prevents a common cheat: the investigation target following a hidden player after the noise. Recording identity and time prevents one animation from becoming several contradictory clues. Recording kind and intensity lets the listener treat a routine footstep differently from an alarm without consulting an invisible narrative script.

Memory makes the difference visible. The listener can orient, travel to the source, search the surrounding area, then abandon or revise the belief. Each phase is grounded in what it received.

## Propagation gives architecture meaning

A wall, closed door, ventilation link, or open stairwell can change both risk and opportunity. That makes route choice more than distance. The player may choose a slower soft surface, open a door to create an acoustic link before placing a device, or time a loud objective while machinery masks it.

The propagation model should match the topology players can see. Direct falloff works in an open chamber. A portal graph often explains rooms and doors. Navigation distance can approximate a maze if its links resemble sound paths. Complexity without visible consequence only makes tuning harder.

Geometry, door state, gameplay propagation, and audible presentation need not use identical algorithms, but their qualitative relationship must agree. A visibly sealed bulkhead cannot make a quiet sound easier to hear unless the world communicates a special conduit.

## Commitment creates the usable opening

Reception alone does not make a distraction. If the listener turns its head for a fraction of a second, the player has evidence but no opportunity. If it abandons every goal for any tiny sound, distraction is free and erratic.

A readable response normally includes orientation, travel, and a bounded inspection or search. Priority hysteresis protects that commitment from weaker events. The player can then estimate an opening from the listener's route and the map.

This does not require a guaranteed win. Device placement, surface noise, travel time, and visual reacquisition can still close the route. The important point is that success follows from a measurable commitment rather than a scripted disappearance.

## One language should describe risk and control

When footsteps use one hidden noise system and decoys use an unrelated trigger, the player cannot transfer knowledge. A shared event language creates useful comparisons: sprinting on metal may be stronger than a hand-thrown clicker through a closed door; a heavy machine may mask both; a listener already in pursuit may reject either.

This shared language also improves verification. The same boundary tests can compare sources, surfaces, barriers, and listener states. It discourages one-off exceptions added only to force the showcase route to work.

## Feedback communicates evidence without exposing omniscience

The player needs to know whether an action created noise and whether a listener received it. Movement animation, a subtle event indicator, listener pose, a directional caption, and sound can work together. Feedback should report the same evidence available through the primary presentation.

It should not draw the exact hidden navigation path or display a listener's private threshold unless the game's fiction provides that instrument. Too little information makes failure arbitrary; too much replaces spatial inference with UI obedience.

Accessibility follows the same principle. Captions, haptics, and visual pulses can communicate event kind, approximate strength, and direction without changing AI behavior or requiring audio hardware.

## Common counterexamples

### “Play a loud clip and alert every nearby enemy”

Playback volume is affected by user settings and mixing. A radius check ignores barriers and may not identify one causal event. Both can be valid presentation or prototype pieces, but neither is a complete hearing model.

### “After hearing anything, chase the player”

This gives the listener knowledge the event did not contain. It erases the distinction between investigation and confirmed pursuit, so deliberate distraction cannot work honestly.

### “Pick a random sound from the queue”

Unexplained priority makes repeated conditions unreliable. Deterministic priority with declared ties and commitment creates learnable behavior; bounded variation can be added after that foundation works.

### “The device is reusable without cost”

Unlimited immediate events can hold a listener forever. Retrieval, cadence, charge, wind-up, or placement risk makes each use a decision rather than a permanent disable button.

### “The enemy walked over there, so hearing works”

It may have followed a scripted point or live player transform. Evidence must show event identity, propagation, reception, stored origin, and selected behavior across time.

## What a fair outcome sounds like in the player's explanation

A player should be able to say, “The closed door weakened my footsteps, but the device beside the open vent carried into the next room,” or “The listener was already looking at me, so the click did not override that evidence.” Such explanations show that the map, event, and listener memory formed one understandable system.
