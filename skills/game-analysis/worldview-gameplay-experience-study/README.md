# Gameplay Experience Study

Turn timecoded gameplay evidence into a readable qualitative case study: what the player expected, what they felt able to do, what surprised them, what they learned, and why a moment was memorable, fair, weak, funny, beautiful, or merely noisy. Then convert that experience into an original design transfer card.

## Call it

```text
/worldview-gameplay-experience-study

Use the pilot evidence and selected experience studies to explain why this
horror sequence stays in the player's memory. Write both a first-person
reconstruction and a third-person design critique. Separate what was observed
from what is reconstructed, identify what was weak or unfair, and transfer only
the player-facing relationship into an original chase route for my project.
```

## Use it when

| Situation | What it adds |
| --- | --- |
| A video report feels technically correct but emotionally dry | Reconstructs the player's attention, expectation, hesitation, choice, surprise, and after-effect before naming a mechanism. |
| A designer wants to know why a scene works, not just what it contains | Judges placement, fairness, memorability, dead time, and the detail that changed the player's prediction. |
| A new horror scene needs references without copying a game | Loads two to five de-identified qualitative cases and transfers relationships, not names, maps, dialogue, UI, or distinctive surfaces. |
| A visual reference board is needed | Routes to original topology diagrams, generated studies, or rights-cleared references and records what each image is allowed to influence. |

## What it returns

- a narrated case study with a first-person player reconstruction and a third-person critic voice;
- an expected-versus-actual turn for each important moment;
- explicit quality judgments with reasons: effective, weak, unfair, memorable, forgettable, funny, beautiful, or merely loud;
- a record of what the player learned, what remained unknown, and what the footage cannot prove;
- a transfer card that preserves a player-facing relationship while changing the setting, story, layout, characters, and surface expression;
- an optional reference board or abstract route plate with ownership and use limits;
- a verification plan for the new game, including what would falsify the transfer.

## Read the case archive selectively

Before inventing a design lesson, read [`references/experience-studies/index.md`](references/experience-studies/index.md). Select the two to five cases with the strongest tag overlap and one counterexample when available. Read each selected `case-study.md` and `transfer-card.md`; open only the visual references listed by that case. If the index has no match, say `no prior case loaded` instead of pretending the Agent has a memory of a game.

The archive is qualitative research, not a gallery of source media. Public cases use de-identified study IDs and original or rights-cleared diagrams. Do not add downloaded videos, raw transcripts, ripped frames, source URLs, recognizable game names, copied layouts, or distinctive dialogue to the archive.

## Content warning

Horror studies may discuss intense audio, flashing or low-light imagery, pursuit, simulated injury, claustrophobia, and distressing scenes. Use your own judgment and stop if the material is unsafe for you. Worldview Skills is a design reference and accepts no responsibility for a user's physical or psychological response, project content, or implementation outcome.
